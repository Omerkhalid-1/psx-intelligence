01 · OWNERSHIP

A share is a slice of a company

A company divides its ownership into millions of equal pieces called shares. Listed companies trade those pieces on the Pakistan Stock Exchange (PSX), formed in 2016 by merging the Karachi, Lahore and Islamabad exchanges.

The KSE-100 index follows 100 large PSX companies. When people say “the market rose”, they usually mean this index.

illustration

Each slice is one share. Owning more shares means owning more of the company.

02 · PRICE

Price is where a buyer meets a seller

Buyers post bids (the most they will pay); sellers post asks (the least they will accept). A trade happens when someone accepts the other side. The last trade's price is “the price”.

More eager buyers lift the price; more eager sellers push it down. Indicators study the trail these trades leave behind.

illustration · not real orders

Teal = bids, orange = asks. Each flash in the middle is a trade, and the line below is the price it creates.

03 · CANDLES

A whole day folds into one candle

Thousands of trades in a session are summarised by four numbers: open (first price), high, low and close (last price). Drawn as a candle, the body spans open to close and the thin wicks reach the high and low.

On this site a hollow candle closed higher than it opened; a filled one closed lower.

04 · VOLUME

Volume: how many shares changed hands

Volume tells you how much conviction is behind a move. A big price change on heavy volume involves many participants; the same change on thin volume involves few.

Relative volume compares a day with its 20-day average. The glowing bars are days with more than twice the usual volume.

05 · TREND

Moving averages reveal the trend

Daily prices are noisy. A moving average replaces each day with the average of the last n closes, so the direction shows through. Short averages react fast; long ones are smooth but slow.

Real closes (blue) and the moving average you chose (magenta).

06 · MOMENTUM

RSI: are gains or losses winning?

The Relative Strength Index compares the average size of recent up-days with recent down-days, on a 0–100 scale. Above 50, gains have been bigger; above 70 the move is stretched (“overbought”); below 30 stretched down (“oversold”).

The bars use plain 14-day averages to show the idea; the engine's official RSI uses Wilder's smoothing, which also weighs older days.

07 · ACCELERATION

MACD: two averages racing

MACD subtracts a slow (26-day) exponential average from a fast (12-day) one. When the fast one pulls away upward, momentum is building; the histogram shows whether that gap is widening or shrinking compared with its own 9-day average.

Real data, last 250 sessions: MACD (blue), signal (amber), histogram.

08 · VOLATILITY

Volatility: how wide the swings are

ATR (Average True Range) is the typical daily range, gaps included. Bollinger Bands sit two standard deviations above and below the 20-day average: they widen in turbulent periods and pinch together in quiet ones.

Real closes inside their Bollinger Bands (violet), last 250 sessions.

09 · MEMORY

Support & resistance: where prices turned before

Prices often pause near levels where they reversed in the past, because many traders remember them. The engine clusters past swing highs and lows into zones: support below the price, resistance above.

Real closes with the engine's nearest zones (teal support, orange resistance).

10 · CONTEXT

Relative strength: the stock versus the market

A stock can fall less than the market and still be a relative winner. Rebasing both to 0% a year ago shows who led. Beta measures how strongly the stock tends to move with the KSE-100.

Real data: stock (blue) vs KSE-100 (magenta), % change over the last 250 sessions.

11 · RISK

Drawdown: distance from the peak

Drawdown is how far the price sits below its highest close. It is the pain a holder from the top would feel. Losses compound unevenly: a 50% fall needs a 100% rise to recover.

Real data: % below the all-time stored peak.

12 · TOGETHER

Putting it together, honestly

The engine turns each directional indicator into positive, neutral or negative and counts them into an indicator balance. It is a tidy summary of what already happened.

It is not a forecast. When this project tested models built on these same indicators against simply assuming “no change” (walk-forward, out-of-sample), they did not do better. Use indicators to understand, not to predict.
